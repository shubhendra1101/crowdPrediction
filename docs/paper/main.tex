\begin{abstract}
Crowd safety depends on how many people stand on each square metre of floor, while most camera systems only count people in the whole image. CrowdSafe turns the video of a fixed CCTV camera into crowd density, in \pmsq, for every zone of the floor and raises alerts before a zone becomes overcrowded. First, a camera model is fitted to desks of known size, which links every image pixel to a floor position in metres. Next, the CLIP-EBC model produces a density map of the people in each frame, and RAFT optical flow measures how the people move. The camera model then converts these maps into the density and walking speed of every floor zone, once per second. The Chronos-2 forecasting model predicts the density of each zone 10--60\,s ahead, and conformal calibration adds a safe upper limit to each forecast. This upper limit places every zone in one of four levels, Low, Medium, High or Critical, and an alert is raised when a zone stays at High or above for 10\,s. The system is tested on classroom CCTV recordings with 6,715 hand-labelled heads, on real crowd experiments from J\"ulich and on JuPedSim simulations. People are counted with an error of 2.0--5.1 per frame, zone density is measured within 0.08--0.10\,\pmsq, the upper limit holds for 84--96\,\% of forecasts, alerts arrive a median of 7.5\,s ahead for 34\,\% of simulated dangerous events, and the full pipeline runs on one A100 GPU with a median delay of 0.49\,s.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Crowd disasters are caused by local density, not by the total number of people present. Dangerous conditions begin at roughly 4--5\,\pmsq, with stop-and-go and turbulent flow at higher densities~\cite{fruin1971,helbing2007}. Many venues already operate fixed CCTV cameras.
Crowd-counting methods and most deployed analytics report people per image~\cite{zhang2016mcnn,li2018csrnet,ma2025clipebc}. Such a count does not show \emph{where} density builds up and cannot be compared with density limits without the floor area that each image region covers, which requires a camera calibration that existing installations rarely have. Anticipating danger also needs a forecaster that works without venue-specific training data, because dangerous events are rare.
Given an existing CCTV camera and a few measured dimensions in the scene, the task is to estimate the density of each floor zone every second, forecast that density with a reliable upper bound, and raise an alert \emph{before} a zone becomes dangerous, with few false alarms.
The objectives are (i) zone density in \pmsq{} without training a counting model; (ii) a forecast bound with coverage checked on separate test data; (iii) alert lead time and false alarms measured on real and simulated dense crowds; and (iv) live latency on one GPU. The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A camera calibration that fits a camera model with lens distortion to desk tops of known length and to straight edges in the room, so that every image pixel maps to a floor position in metres; the result is checked against tape-measured distances that are not used in the fit.
\item A counting evaluation on real CCTV across rooms, which selects a zero-shot density model over person detectors and detector--density combinations.
\item Zero-shot forecasting of zone density with a conformally calibrated upper bound.
\item An early-warning evaluation with a clear lead-time protocol on real and simulated dense crowds.
\item A live implementation with measured latency and a replay dashboard.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Crowd Counting: Detection versus Density Estimation}
Density-map methods estimate a per-pixel density whose sum is the count. MCNN introduced the ShanghaiTech benchmark~\cite{zhang2016mcnn}. CSRNet replaced pooling with dilated convolutions~\cite{li2018csrnet}. CLIP-EBC recasts counting as blockwise classification on a CLIP backbone trained on NWPU-Crowd~\cite{ma2025clipebc,wang2021nwpu}. Detectors such as YOLO11, including models trained on CrowdHuman, count only visible people~\cite{yolo11,shao2018crowdhuman}.

\subsection{Crowd Dynamics and Danger Indicators}
Video of the 2006 Hajj disaster shows a transition from laminar to stop-and-go to turbulent flow, and crowd pressure, defined as density times velocity variance, is proposed as a danger indicator~\cite{helbing2007}. The J\"ulich archive provides trajectories of controlled high-density experiments~\cite{julich_archive}. PedPy analyses and JuPedSim simulates such pedestrian data~\cite{pedpy,jupedsim}.

\subsection{Crowd Density and Flow Forecasting}
ST-ResNet forecasts the inflow and outflow of city regions~\cite{zhang2017stresnet}. A two-stream recurrent network forecasts future crowd distributions from video~\cite{niu2020overcrowdedness}. Chronos and Chronos-2 forecast new series without training~\cite{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}. Split conformal prediction gives a bound with finite-sample coverage~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}.

\subsection{Integrated Crowd Monitoring and Early-Warning Systems}
A video-based system estimates and predicts crowd density over a multi-camera network for wide-area surveillance~\cite{cao2013video}. Forecasting of future crowd distributions is also motivated by alerts for regions that will become over-crowded~\cite{niu2020overcrowdedness}.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:gap} summarises the reviewed studies. The reviewed sources contain no evaluation that combines density in \pmsq{} per floor zone from an existing uncalibrated camera, a forecast bound with verified coverage, and alert lead time measured on real dense crowds. CrowdSafe covers this combination with pretrained components only.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
CrowdSafe is a chain of steps that turns camera frames into alerts, and each step works on the output of the previous one, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. A camera model, fitted once, relates image pixels to the floor. Every second, a frame becomes a map of where people are, and a pair of frames becomes a field of how people move; both are collected per floor zone into densities in \pmsq{} and motion statistics, stored as one row per zone and second. Every 5\,s, the recent rows of each zone are forecast, the calibrated upper bound of the forecast becomes a risk level, and sustained high levels become alerts on a dashboard. Each subsection states the operation of one step and gives an example from the real CCTV, J\"ulich and simulated data.

\subsection{Camera Calibration}
Density in \pmsq{} requires knowing which floor area each part of the image shows. CrowdSafe fits a camera model, namely a pinhole camera with radial lens distortion ($k_1,k_2$), focal length, position and tilt, by robust least squares to a few objects of known size in a still frame and to straight edges, which must appear straight once the distortion is removed. A tape measure is the only extra resource. The fitted model carries any pixel to a point on the floor at a chosen height, which the zone and motion steps rely on.
In the classrooms, desk tops of measured length (1.00\,m) serve as rectangles, with each desk position left free so that the row and column spacing remain independent checks, and door frames and the ceiling duct serve as straight edges. In the computer lab, monitors hide most desk tops, so long table edges at measured positions, vertical edges and the measured camera height are used instead.

\subsection{Zone Definition and Area Computation}
The floor is then divided into zones on a plan in metres, small enough that a dangerous pocket is not averaged away by an empty corner. The camera model projects each zone into the image at the height where heads appear, which gives the image region $M_z$ that belongs to zone $z$ and the floor area $A_z$ that the camera actually sees. These two quantities later turn a count of people into a density.
Each classroom uses 3$\times$3 seating blocks plus the front aisle (4--16\,m$^2$ each), projected at seated head height (1.2\,m); in the computer lab, a glass partition that shows the neighbouring room is masked out.

\subsection{People Counting}
Every second, a video frame passes to a pretrained density model, CLIP-EBC ViT-B/16 with NWPU-Crowd weights, used without any training~\cite{ma2025clipebc,wang2021nwpu}. Applied in non-overlapping 224-pixel windows (45 windows for a zero-padded 1080p frame), the model returns a density map $D_t$ of 135$\times$240 cells, one per 8$\times$8 pixels, whose values are expected numbers of heads, so the map can be summed over any image region. A density model is chosen over person detectors because detectors count only the people visible as separate boxes, and desks, monitors and other people hide many bodies in seated rooms. Combinations of detector and density-model frame counts serve as further baselines: a hard switch to the detector below a threshold $\tau$, a constant or 50/50 weighting, and a learned per-frame weight. The detectors are YOLO11l and a YOLO11 head detector trained on CrowdHuman~\cite{yolo11,shao2018crowdhuman}.
In the recordings, one frame per second of every classroom and lab video passes through CLIP-EBC; the detectors and combinations run on the 160 hand-labelled frames to select the counting route, as reported in Section~\ref{sec:count}.

\subsection{Zone Density Estimation in Persons per \texorpdfstring{m$^2$}{m2}}
The density map from the counting step meets the zone regions and areas from the zone step. Summing the map inside a zone region gives the count $c_{z,t}=\sum D_t\odot M_z$, and dividing by the visible area gives the density $\rho_{z,t}=c_{z,t}/A_z$ in \pmsq. Zones with less than 2\,m$^2$ in view are skipped, because the density of such small areas is unstable. This per-second density is the quantity that the rest of the pipeline monitors.
For one classroom, this step produces ten densities every second, one for each seating block and the aisle.

\subsection{Crowd Motion Feature Extraction}
Density shows how full a zone is; motion shows how the crowd behaves. The same sampled frame and a second frame 0.2\,s later pass to pretrained RAFT-large optical flow at 544$\times$960 pixels with 12 refinement iterations, and the flow is averaged into the same 135$\times$240 cells as $D_t$~\cite{teed2020raft}. The camera model carries each displacement to the floor, turning pixel shifts into velocities in m/s, weighted by the density map so that empty floor contributes nothing. Per zone, this gives the mean speed, the velocity variance, crowd pressure $\rho\cdot\mathrm{var}$, the entropy of an 8-bin direction histogram and the share of counter-flow~\cite{helbing2007}. The number of people crossing a zone boundary per second (inflow and outflow) needs individual trajectories, so these values exist only where trajectories exist.
For the real CCTV data, all motion values come from RAFT and inflow and outflow remain empty; for the J\"ulich and simulated data, speeds and boundary crossings come directly from each trajectory.

\subsection{Zone Feature Table Construction}
All measurements meet in a single table with one row per camera (or run), zone and second, holding count, density, the motion values, inflow, outflow and the data source. Forecasting and alerting read only this table, so every source that fills the table is evaluated in exactly the same way.
The real CCTV data fill the table through the density and motion steps. The J\"ulich and simulated data fill the table directly, with 2\,m$\times$2\,m floor cells as zones, and the J\"ulich densities agree exactly with PedPy on six checked runs~\cite{pedpy}. Dense crowds absent from the classroom recordings therefore pass through the same downstream steps.

\subsection{Crowd Density Forecasting}
Every 5\,s, the last 30\,s of each zone are read from the table as a regular 1-Hz series and forecast 10, 30 and 60\,s ahead. The forecaster is Chronos-2, a pretrained time-series model used zero-shot~\cite{ansari2025chronos2}. A zero-shot model is chosen because dangerous crowd events are too rare to train a forecaster on venue data. The model runs either \emph{per zone}, with pressure, inflow, outflow and mean speed as additional inputs (set to zero when missing), or \emph{jointly} over all zones of one camera so that neighbouring zones inform each other, and returns the 10, 50 and 90\,\% quantiles for each zone and horizon. Two simple forecasters serve as baselines: \emph{persistence}, which repeats the last value, and a \emph{physics} fill-rate model, $\rho+(q_{\mathrm{in}}-q_{\mathrm{out}})\,h/A_z$, which needs inflow and outflow. In live operation, a second skipped by a slow cycle is filled with the previous value so that the series stays regular. The horizon limits how early a warning can arrive, but the horizon is not the warning time itself.
Because most J\"ulich runs last only 1--3\,min, horizons of 10--60\,s apply there and on the real CCTV data, with 120\,s added on the longer simulations; the physics model runs only on the J\"ulich and simulated data.

\subsection{Reliable Upper Bound via Conformal Calibration}
The 90\,\% quantile of a forecaster carries no guarantee of being exceeded only 10\,\% of the time, and point forecasts have no spread at all. CrowdSafe therefore calibrates the upper bound with split conformal prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}. On validation runs, the amount by which the realised density rose above the 90\,\% quantile is measured, and the finite-sample 90\,\% quantile of these residuals becomes an offset $q_h$ for each horizon. New forecasts then carry the bound $U_{z,h}=\max(0,\,\mathrm{p90}_{z,h}+q_h)$, the worst case on which the risk step operates.
The offsets are fitted on two validation cameras, 39 J\"ulich validation runs and four validation scenarios, and applied only to the test data. On the real CCTV data, the Chronos-2 offsets are tiny (0.001--0.02\,\pmsq), while for persistence the offset of 0.14--0.18\,\pmsq{} forms the whole bound.

\subsection{Risk Classification (Low, Medium, High, Critical)}
The calibrated bounds over all horizons and the current density combine into a worst case, $W_z=\max(\max_h U_{z,h},\,\rho_z)$, mapped to Low ($<2$), Medium (2--4), High (4--5) or Critical ($\geq 5$\,\pmsq). The first horizon whose bound reaches 5\,\pmsq{} is reported as the time to critical. Before the first forecast of a run, only the current density is used.
In a classroom recording, a zone at 1.6\,\pmsq{} appears as Medium because the forecast bound of that zone exceeds 2\,\pmsq, as visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard}.

\subsection{Alert Generation}
Levels change every 5\,s and can flicker, so an alert manager adds hysteresis: an alert is raised when a zone stays High or above for 10\,s and cleared once the zone has stayed below High for 30\,s. Each alert records the zone, the highest level reached, the time to critical and a short reason, such as ``forecast upper bound 4.2 \pmsq{} (now 1.8)''.
In a J\"ulich corridor run, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline}, the bound of one zone crosses 4\,\pmsq{} and an alert is raised at 219\,s, 49\,s before the zone itself reaches 4\,\pmsq.

\subsection{Dashboard Visualisation}
The dashboard presents the zones, levels, forecasts and alerts to an operator. A FastAPI/WebSocket server replays a stored run by re-running the risk and alert steps on the feature rows and forecasts of the run, and streams one message per second to a Next.js dashboard: the camera video with zones coloured by level and labelled with density, the forecast of a selected zone and the alert feed. The live pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:runtime} runs the same steps directly on a camera stream.
Fig.~\ref{fig:dashboard} shows the dashboard replaying a classroom recording.

\subsection{Real CCTV Data}
Two classrooms and a computer lab are recorded with permission by four fixed cameras (2.6\,h, 1080p). Head points on 160 frames are annotated by correcting model pre-labels. The busiest zone reaches 3.2\,\pmsq, so the real CCTV data contain no dangerous density. No faces or identities are stored.

\subsection{ShanghaiTech Benchmark}
ShanghaiTech A and B are standard counting benchmarks, and only the test sets serve here to check the zero-shot models~\cite{zhang2016mcnn,li2018csrnet}.

\subsection{J\"ulich Data}
The first test experiment is a unidirectional corridor, previously analysed for fundamental diagrams~\cite{julich_uni2013,cao2017fundamental}. The second is an entrance crowd, previously studied for collective crowd behaviour~\cite{julich_semicircle,sieben2017collective}. The two experiments reach 5.3 and 9.0\,\pmsq.

\subsection{Simulated Data}
Sixteen scenarios in four layouts (classroom, corridor bottleneck, two-exit hall, bidirectional corridor) include arrival surges and exit closures. Desired walking speeds follow $\mathcal{N}(1.34, 0.26^2)$\,m/s~\cite{weidmann1993}. Simulations follow model rules, not real behaviour.

\subsection{Train, Validation, and Test Splits}
No model is trained. Counting combinations are fitted on two rooms and tested on the third, in turn. Conformal offsets use validation cameras, experiments or scenarios and are applied to separate test ones. Test data serve no fitting.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Counting uses the mean absolute error (MAE) in people per frame, reported in Table~\ref{tab:counting} and Table~\ref{tab:fusion}, and zone density uses the MAE in \pmsq. Forecasts use the MAE and the \emph{coverage}, the share of test forecasts at or below the bound (target 0.90), reported in Table~\ref{tab:forecast}. For alerts, an \emph{event} is the first second at which a zone reaches 4 (or 5)\,\pmsq{} after monitoring starts. An event counts as \emph{warned in time} if an alert is raised at most (longest horizon + 10\,s) before the event and more than 15\,s after monitoring begins. Lead time is the time from alert to event, and a \emph{false alarm} is an alert with no event within 120\,s, counted per zone-hour; Table~\ref{tab:alerts} reports both. Runtime is the median time from frame arrival to alert state, reported with model sizes in Table~\ref{tab:models}.

\subsection{Hardware and Software}
Density maps, flow, Chronos-2 and latency run on one shared NVIDIA A100 (40\,GB); label-based counting uses ONNX models on a CPU. Simulations use JuPedSim 1.4.2 and analysis uses PedPy 1.5.1. No model is trained; the deployed path has about 222\,M parameters, feature extraction for all 2.6\,h of real CCTV data takes 33.5\,min on the A100, and the 16 simulations take 9.7\,min on a CPU.

\subsection{Counting Accuracy on CCTV and Model Selection}
\label{sec:count}

\subsection{Calibration Accuracy}
Reprojection errors are 3.6--4.2\,px. On distances excluded from the fit, the row spacing of the first classroom measures 0.99 against 0.96\,m (+3\,\%) and the mean desk spacing differs by $-5$\,\%; a door in the second classroom measures 1.23 against 1.12\,m (+10\,\%); and table edges in the computer lab lie within 0.03--0.18\,m. The calibration is therefore accurate to about $\pm 5$\,\%.

\subsection{Zone Density Accuracy}
Zone-density MAE is 0.101 and 0.081\,\pmsq{} in the two classrooms and 0.078\,\pmsq{} in the computer lab, with 94--98\,\% of heads inside a zone, and changing the head height from 1.1 to 1.6\,m alters the MAE by at most 0.03. These errors are about 20 times smaller than the 2\,\pmsq{} width of each risk band.

\subsection{Forecasting Accuracy and Upper-Bound Reliability}
\label{sec:forecast}
Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports forecasting results on the test splits. \subsection{Early-Warning Performance}
Table~\ref{tab:alerts} reports the alert results. Alerts on current density alone give no warning before a zone reaches 4\,\pmsq, which shows the value of forecasting. Forecast-driven alerts warn in time for 34\,\% of the 47 simulated events with Chronos-2 (median 7.5\,s ahead, 1.6 false alarms per zone-hour) and for 32\,\% with the physics model (median 25\,s ahead, 2.1 false alarms per zone-hour). On the J\"ulich data, the Chronos-2 per-zone forecaster warns 49\,s before the zone reaches 4\,\pmsq{}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline}. For a 5\,\pmsq{} event, alerts warn for 38\,\% of 34 simulated events. The real CCTV data produce no events and no false alarms in 8.6 zone-hours. Zones fill from safe to 4\,\pmsq{} in about 10--20\,s, which sets the scale of the lead times.

\label{sec:runtime}

Unpaced, 908\,s of video take 196\,s (4.6$\times$ real time). \subsection{Comparison with Existing Systems}
On ShanghaiTech A, zero-shot CLIP-EBC reaches a lower error than CSRNet and MCNN, both trained on that dataset (Table~\ref{tab:counting}), so CrowdSafe obtains density maps without any training data. CrowdSafe reports density in \pmsq{} for every floor zone with an error of 0.08--0.10\,\pmsq. Image-level counters report only people per image~\cite{zhang2016mcnn,li2018csrnet}. System-level studies use different data and metrics, so a numerical comparison is not possible~\cite{cao2013video,niu2020overcrowdedness}.
The evaluation covers seated rooms for CCTV, so dense-crowd results rest on nine real J\"ulich events and on simulated data, and the calibration assumes seated head height.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusion}
CrowdSafe turns an existing CCTV camera into a crowd-density monitor for every floor zone without training any model. People are counted with an error of 2.0--5.1 per frame, up to nine times lower than a person detector, and zone density is measured within 0.08--0.10\,\pmsq. The calibrated forecast bound holds for 84--96\,\% of forecasts and stays up to 20 percentage points closer to target than persistence in dense crowds. Forecast-driven alerts arrive 7.5\,s ahead (median) on simulated data and up to 49\,s ahead on real J\"ulich data, where alerts on current density give no warning, and the full pipeline answers in 0.49\,s on one GPU.

\subsection{Future Work}
The warning lead time of a single camera is bounded by the area in view. Counts from entrance cameras and the inflow measured across zone boundaries from CCTV, added as forecaster inputs, extend the warning horizon beyond the field of view. Evaluation on real CCTV recordings of dense crowds, with several cameras processed on one GPU, extends the validation beyond seated rooms and measures the multi-camera capacity of the pipeline.
